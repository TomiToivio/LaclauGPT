### Gleb Papyshev & Ninell Oldenburg — *The Policy Umwelt: How AI Problem Representations Render Governance Alternatives Unthinkable* (2026)

- **Venue / publisher:** *The International Journal of Press/Politics*, Special Issue: “Global Myth and Magic around AI”
- **DOI / URL:** https://doi.org/10.1177/19401612261474295
- **Local PDF filename:** `papyshev-oldenburg-2026-the-policy-umwelt-how-ai-problem-representations-render-governance-alternatives-unthinkable.pdf`
- **Topics:** AI governance; AI narratives; existential risk; accelerationism; Critical AI; policy problem representation; WPR; sociotechnical imaginaries; technological determinism; governance alternatives
- **Status:** summarized
- **LaclauGPT relevance:** Very high. The paper independently compares existential-risk, accelerationist, and Critical AI narratives as competing problem representations and shows how each makes some governance options thinkable while excluding others. This closely parallels LaclauGPT’s analysis of provisional ideological formations, contested signifiers, articulations, frontiers, and sociotechnical imaginaries, while adding a useful emphasis on structured absences and the governance options a discourse renders difficult to perceive.

### Stephen Cave & Kanta Dihal (eds.) — *Imagining AI: How the World Sees Intelligent Machines* (2023)

- **Venue / publisher:** Oxford University Press
- **DOI / URL:** https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780192865366.001.0001
- **Local PDF filename:** `9780192865366.pdf`
- **Topics:** AI narratives; sociotechnical imaginaries; cross-cultural comparison; AI ideology; mythology; science fiction; policy discourse; colonialism; decolonial AI; developmentalism; existential risk; language; cultural politics; Global South
- **Status:** summarized
- **LaclauGPT relevance:** Very high. The volume treats AI as simultaneously technological and cultural, arguing that fictional and non-fictional narratives co-produce sociotechnical imaginaries that shape public perception, policy, funding, design, and technological development. Its cross-cultural cases show that “AI” is not one stable global object but is articulated differently through language, history, political economy, religion, colonial legacies, development strategies, and cultural traditions. This makes the book especially useful for AI26’s analysis of contested signifiers, ideological formations, national variation, and the relationship between elite, policy, and grassroots imaginaries. Particularly relevant chapters include the cross-cultural analysis of the term “AI,” the American utopia/dystopia and loss-of-control imaginary, Abeba Birhane’s critique of algorithmic colonialism in Africa, and So Young Kim’s analysis of South Korean developmental-state AI policy discourse.
