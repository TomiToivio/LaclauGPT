### Gleb Papyshev & Ninell Oldenburg — *The Policy Umwelt: How AI Problem Representations Render Governance Alternatives Unthinkable* (2026)

**Bibliographic note:** *The International Journal of Press/Politics*, Special Issue “Global Myth and Magic around AI.” DOI: `10.1177/19401612261474295`.

**Core argument / research question:** The paper asks how AI narratives do more than influence policy preferences. Its central claim is that problem representations prefigure the field of politically thinkable intervention. The authors call this constrained perceptual/action space a **Policy Umwelt**, adapting Jakob von Uexküll’s concept. A narrative makes some interventions appear obvious, urgent, or rational while making others difficult to perceive as legitimate options.

**Methods / material:** The authors apply Carol Bacchi’s “What’s the Problem Represented to Be?” (WPR) framework to three deliberately selected manifesto-style texts: MIRI’s *The Problem* as an existential-risk case, Marc Andreessen’s *The Techno-Optimist Manifesto* as an accelerationist case, and DAIR’s *Research Philosophy* as a Critical AI case. The six WPR questions serve as a priori structural codes. Two researchers coded the texts independently and resolved discrepancies through consensus.

**Key findings or claims:**

- **Existential risk / MIRI:** AI is represented as an impending misaligned-superintelligence catastrophe. The resulting Umwelt privileges global technocratic containment, moratoria, international control, and technical alignment work, while tending to marginalize present-day harms, democratic participation, political economy, and non-catastrophic visions of advanced AI.
- **Accelerationism / Andreessen:** The problem becomes obstruction of technological progress. Markets, builders, growth, abundance, and acceleration are privileged, while regulation, precaution, redistribution, structural inequality, environmental costs, and concentrated private power are pushed outside the legitimate policy field.
- **Critical AI / DAIR:** AI is represented as a present system of structural injustice, extraction, and epistemic exclusion. This foregrounds community-led research, participatory design, labor rights, redistribution, refusal, and epistemic justice, while making industry compromise, competitiveness-oriented policy, and incremental market-friendly reform harder to recognize as legitimate.
- The three narratives are not equivalent. MIRI and Andreessen rely strongly on technological or market determinism, whereas DAIR explicitly treats AI trajectories as politically contingent. Their deeper similarity is that each still constructs a selective field of visibility and action.
- The paper argues that all three tend to make **pragmatic, experimental governance** less visible: iterative policy learning, pilot programs, empirical feedback, institutional flexibility, ex-post accountability, and revisability rather than commitment to a predetermined technological future.
- Policy Umwelt is presented as a general feature of policy discourse, not a pathology unique to extreme positions. Every coherent problem representation foregrounds some possibilities and backgrounds others.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** The most useful contribution is the distinction between explicit advocacy and **prefiguration**. A discourse does not merely rank known policy choices; it helps constitute which choices enter the field of legitimate consideration at all. This gives LaclauGPT a strong complementary concept for analyzing structured absences, silences, exclusions, and “unthinkable” alternatives. It also offers a bridge between ideological formations and concrete governance consequences. “Policy Umwelt” could be treated as a higher-level corpus interpretation derived from recurring problem representations, articulations, demands, agents of change, and excluded alternatives rather than as a document-level classifier.

**Relevance to `paper/PAPER.md`:** This paper is unusually close to LaclauGPT’s empirical and theoretical target. Its existential-risk, accelerationist, and Critical AI cases overlap directly with the project’s provisional formation space. Both approaches reject the idea that political language simply describes a pre-existing technological reality and instead analyze how discourse constructs problems, subjects, futures, and legitimate interventions. For LaclauGPT, WPR/Policy Umwelt can complement Laclau and Mouffe without replacing them: Laclaudian analysis can model signifiers, articulations, equivalence/difference, frontiers, subjects, affects, and hegemonic contestation, while Policy Umwelt draws attention to the governance horizon generated by those articulations and especially to structured silences. The paper also reinforces the project’s decision not to assign one permanent ideology to an actor: overlapping policy proposals can arise from radically different problem representations and normative chains.

**Limitations / cautions:** The empirical base is intentionally narrow: three ideal-typical manifestos chosen for theory-building rather than statistical representativeness. The authors explicitly warn that this may exaggerate coherence, understate hybrid positions and internal contradictions, and conflate features of manifesto rhetoric with broader ideological traditions. They do not demonstrate how Policy Umwelten translate into actual policy decisions. The concept can also appear more static than real contested discourse, where counter-narratives and events continually reopen what is thinkable. Finally, their proposed pragmatic alternative is itself normatively situated and may be inadequate for unprecedented or irreversible harms where waiting for empirical feedback could be too late.

**Potential follow-up:** Add Policy Umwelt/WPR as a comparative concept in the literature review or discussion, especially for the transition from discourse formation to governance implications. Consider a LaclauGPT-derived corpus measure or researcher memo for `problem representation → privileged interventions → excluded/silent alternatives`, but keep this as a human-validated corpus-level interpretation rather than automatic classification. The three manifesto cases also provide useful benchmark material for testing whether the pipeline can distinguish similar policy instruments justified through different ideological articulations.

### Stephen Cave & Kanta Dihal (eds.) — *Imagining AI: How the World Sees Intelligent Machines* (2023)

**Bibliographic note:** Oxford University Press. ISBN `978-0-19-286536-6`. DOI: `10.1093/oso/9780192865366.001.0001`. Edited collection arising from the Global AI Narratives project at the Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence, University of Cambridge.

**Core argument / research question:** The collection asks how intelligent machines are imagined across different cultures and regions, and how those historically specific narratives shape the meanings, hopes, fears, policies, and technological trajectories associated with AI. Cave and Dihal argue that AI was a cultural phenomenon before it was a technological category and that contemporary AI remains entangled with long-standing myths and fantasies. The volume therefore rejects the assumption that there is a single universal “AI imaginary.” Instead, AI is continually reinterpreted through language, history, ideology, religion, political economy, colonial experience, literature, popular culture, and national development projects.

**Methods / material:** This is an interdisciplinary edited collection rather than a single-method empirical study. It grew from twenty Global AI Narratives workshops conducted from 2018 to 2021 with collaborators across six continents. Chapters analyze heterogeneous material including myths, literature, science fiction, comics, visual art, film, government propaganda, policy documents, technological discourse, and ethnographic observations. The book is organized geographically across Europe; the Americas and Pacific; Africa, the Middle East and South Asia; and East and South East Asia. The editors explicitly refuse a strict fiction/non-fiction divide because both are treated as components of sociotechnical imaginaries.

**Key findings or claims:**

- **AI is culturally constituted as well as technically produced.** The category “artificial intelligence” rests on older fantasies of intelligent machines, and contemporary technological development interacts continuously with mythology and narrative. Academia, industry, media, culture, and policy form mutually influential sites of AI production.
- **AI narratives form sociotechnical imaginaries.** Fictional and non-fictional representations jointly influence public perception and the direction of technological development. Narratives therefore matter to funding, engineering goals, product design, adoption, regulation, and governance rather than merely reflecting technological change after the fact.
- **The signifier “AI” travels unevenly across languages.** The cross-cultural terminology chapter traces “artificial” and “intelligence” across Germanic, Romance, Slavonic, Japanese, and Chinese contexts. Terms carry different histories and value connotations, so translation does not simply reproduce an invariant technological concept. This is especially important for discourse analysis because apparently equivalent labels can occupy different semantic and ideological positions.
- **The American AI imaginary is structured by an unstable utopia/dystopia relation.** US narratives repeatedly imagine AI as a route to mastery over nature, mortality, scarcity, and human limitation, while the same imagined superhuman power generates fears of loss of control, enslavement, or extinction. The chapter connects these narratives to American histories of technological supremacy, settler colonialism, racial hierarchy, and domination. Utopian technological mastery and catastrophic x-risk can therefore be interpreted as two sides of a shared imaginary rather than wholly independent discourses.
- **Global AI imaginaries cannot be reduced to the American loss-of-control template.** Japanese narratives considered in the volume emphasize animacy, emotion, companionship, and human-machine partnership; contemporary Chinese cases draw on different philosophical traditions and histories; Singaporean narratives often emphasize coexistence and mutual dependence rather than annihilation or enslavement. These examples complicate claims that existential-risk narratives are culturally universal.
- **Developmentalism strongly shapes AI policy discourse.** So Young Kim shows how South Korean AI policy after AlphaGo reproduces older developmental-state structures: state-led planning, technology as an engine of economic growth, global catch-up rhetoric, strategic investment, and industrial competitiveness. Even AI ethics policy is partly articulated through industrial-development priorities. The case demonstrates how a globally circulating signifier such as AI can be inserted into an older national hegemonic project.
- **AI can function as a vehicle of colonial and extractive power.** Abeba Birhane describes algorithmic colonialism as domination exercised through digital ecosystems, infrastructure, data extraction, categorization, and technology monopolies rather than direct territorial rule. Western technology firms can present expansion into Africa through narratives of connection, financial inclusion, development, modernization, and technological solutions while simultaneously concentrating epistemic and infrastructural authority. Birhane stresses that this is not an argument against AI as such, but against imposed business models, universalizing frameworks, and power relations that suppress local knowledge and locally governed alternatives.
- **Decolonial and Indigenous perspectives generate alternative imaginaries rather than merely criticizing Western AI.** Chapters on Afrofuturism in Brazil, Indigenous AI, Maoli Intelligence, African and postcolonial perspectives, and MENA histories foreground counter-narratives, data sovereignty, alternative understandings of intelligence and personhood, resistance to algorithmic racism, and culturally situated technological futures.
- **National and regional imaginaries remain internally heterogeneous.** The editors caution against treating cultures as sealed units. Narratives travel through colonialism, migration, globalization, media, political ideology, and technological exchange. The comparative unit should therefore be treated as historically relational rather than as an essential national “culture.”

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** The volume offers an unusually strong bridge between **sociotechnical imaginaries and Laclaudian discourse analysis**. It supports treating `AI`, `intelligence`, `progress`, `development`, `control`, `risk`, `humanity`, and related terms as historically variable signifiers rather than transparent labels for fixed objects. The cross-linguistic chapter is particularly useful for LLM Structuralism because it demonstrates that semantic relations and evaluative connotations change across linguistic systems. The book also offers numerous candidate articulations for comparative coding: `AI → progress → development → national competitiveness`; `AI → superintelligence → loss of control → extinction`; `AI → infrastructure → extraction → colonialism`; and alternative chains around partnership, care, sovereignty, coexistence, and situated knowledge.

A second important contribution is the idea that **ideological formations can share deep narrative grammar while disagreeing politically**. In the American case, techno-utopian mastery and existential-risk fear both depend on imagining extraordinarily powerful machines and a hierarchy structured by intelligence and control. This suggests an AI26 research question: accelerationist and doomer discourse may sometimes constitute antagonistic positions inside a broader shared American/Californian imaginary rather than completely separate ideological universes.

**Relevance to `paper/PAPER.md`:** Very high. AI26 studies ideological contestation over AI across elite, parliamentary/policy, and grassroots arenas. *Imagining AI* supplies strong comparative evidence that the object being contested is itself culturally and historically constructed. It therefore strengthens the rationale for treating AI/AGI as a potentially floating or tendentially empty signifier whose meaning is produced through articulation rather than assumed in advance. The book also warns against deriving a supposedly global codebook exclusively from US elite discourse: x-risk, accelerationism, Critical AI, developmentalism, decolonial critique, partnership/coexistence imaginaries, Indigenous perspectives, and other formations may overlap differently across countries and languages.

For the empirical design, the volume suggests analyzing both **transnational formations and nationally sedimented imaginaries**. The same signifier can travel globally while becoming articulated to different histories: US frontier/mastery narratives, South Korean developmental-state catch-up, African anti-colonial and infrastructural concerns, Japanese human-machine relationality, or Chinese philosophical and political traditions. This is directly compatible with LaclauGPT’s relational approach because such patterns can be tested as recurring chains, oppositions, nodal points, and frontiers rather than encoded as immutable cultural labels.

**Limitations / cautions:** The volume is deliberately wide-ranging and heterogeneous. Chapters use different disciplines, materials, scales, and evidentiary standards, so their findings should not be treated as one standardized comparative dataset. Coverage is also uneven: the editors explicitly acknowledge stronger representation of some regions, especially Europe and China, and thinner coverage of places such as Sub-Saharan Africa and India. The geographical organization can tempt readers to essentialize national or regional cultures, although the editors themselves repeatedly caution against this. Much of the empirical material predates the post-2022 generative-AI boom, so the book is best used as a genealogy and comparative framework for AI26, not as a description of the current ideological field.

**Potential follow-up:** Use the book to add a cross-cultural/sociotechnical-imaginary layer to AI26 without turning national identity into a fixed classifier. Candidate human-validated corpus questions include: Which older national or cultural narratives are being re-articulated around generative AI and AGI? Do accelerationist and doomer texts share a deeper loss-of-control/mastery grammar? How do `AI`, `intelligence`, `progress`, `risk`, and `development` shift semantically across languages? Does parliamentary AI discourse reproduce national developmental or geopolitical traditions while elite AI discourse remains more transnational? Birhane’s chapter is also an obvious bridge to future Kenya/Africa work, particularly for examining how AI-development discourse articulates infrastructure, dependency, extraction, sovereignty, and locally governed alternatives.
