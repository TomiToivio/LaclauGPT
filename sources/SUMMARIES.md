### Gleb Papyshev & Ninell Oldenburg — *The Policy Umwelt: How AI Problem Representations Render Governance Alternatives Unthinkable* (2026)

**Bibliographic note:** *The International Journal of Press/Politics*, Special Issue “Global Myth and Magic around AI.” DOI: `10.1177/19401612261474295`.

**Core argument / research question:** The paper asks how AI narratives do more than influence policy preferences. Its central claim is that problem representations prefigure the field of politically thinkable intervention. The authors call this constrained perceptual/action space a **Policy Umwelt**, adapting Jakob von Uexküll’s concept. A narrative makes some interventions appear obvious, urgent, or rational while making others difficult to perceive as legitimate options.

**Methods / material:** The authors apply Carol Bacchi’s “What’s the Problem Represented to Be?” (WPR) framework to three deliberately selected manifesto-style texts: MIRI’s *The Problem* as an existential-risk case, Marc Andreessen’s *The Techno-Optimist Manifesto* as an accelerationist case, and DAIR’s *Research Philosophy* as a Critical AI case. The six WPR questions serve as a priori structural codes. Two researchers coded the texts independently and resolved discrepancies through consensus.

**Key findings or claims:**

- **Existential risk / MIRI:** AI is represented as an impending misaligned-superintelligence catastrophe. The resulting Umwelt privileges global technocratic containment, moratoria, international control, and technical alignment work, while tending to marginalize present-day harms, democratic participation, political economy, and non-catastrophic visions of advanced AI.
- **Accelerationism / Andreessen:** The problem becomes obstruction of technological progress. Markets, builders, growth, abundance, and acceleration are privileged, while regulation, precaution, redistribution, structural inequality, environmental costs, and concentrated private power are pushed outside the legitimate policy field.
- **Critical AI / DAIR:** AI is represented as a present system of structural injustice, extraction, and epistemic exclusion. This foregrounds community-led research, participatory design, labor rights, redistribution, refusal, and epistemic justice, while making industry compromise, competitiveness-oriented policy, and incremental market-friendly reform harder to recognize as legitimate.
- The three narratives are not equivalent. MIRI and Andreessen rely strongly on technological or market determinism, whereas DAIR explicitly treats AI trajectories as politically contingent. Their deeper similarity is that each still constructs a selective field of visibility and action.
- The paper argues that all three tend to make **pragmatic, experimental governance** less visible: iterative policy learning, pilot programs, empirical feedback, institutional flexibility, ex-post accountability, and revisability rather than commitment to a predetermined technological future.
- Policy Umwelt is presented as a general feature of policy discourse, not a pathology unique to extreme positions. Every coherent problem representation foregrounds some possibilities and backgrounds others.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** The most useful contribution is the distinction between explicit advocacy and **prefiguration**. A discourse does not merely rank known policy choices; it helps constitute which choices enter the field of legitimate consideration at all. This gives LaclauGPT a strong complementary concept for analyzing structured absences, silences, exclusions, and “unthinkable” alternatives. It also offers a bridge between ideological formations and concrete governance consequences. “Policy Umwelt” could be treated as a higher-level corpus interpretation derived from recurring problem representations, articulations, demands, agents of change, and excluded alternatives rather than as a document-level classifier.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_1_PAPER.md`:** This paper is unusually close to LaclauGPT’s empirical and theoretical target. Its existential-risk, accelerationist, and Critical AI cases overlap directly with the project’s provisional formation space. Both approaches reject the idea that political language simply describes a pre-existing technological reality and instead analyze how discourse constructs problems, subjects, futures, and legitimate interventions. For LaclauGPT, WPR/Policy Umwelt can complement Laclau and Mouffe without replacing them: Laclaudian analysis can model signifiers, articulations, equivalence/difference, frontiers, subjects, affects, and hegemonic contestation, while Policy Umwelt draws attention to the governance horizon generated by those articulations and especially to structured silences. The paper also reinforces the project’s decision not to assign one permanent ideology to an actor: overlapping policy proposals can arise from radically different problem representations and normative chains.

**Limitations / cautions:** The empirical base is intentionally narrow: three ideal-typical manifestos chosen for theory-building rather than statistical representativeness. The authors explicitly warn that this may exaggerate coherence, understate hybrid positions and internal contradictions, and conflate features of manifesto rhetoric with broader ideological traditions. They do not demonstrate how Policy Umwelten translate into actual policy decisions. The concept can also appear more static than real contested discourse, where counter-narratives and events continually reopen what is thinkable. Finally, their proposed pragmatic alternative is itself normatively situated and may be inadequate for unprecedented or irreversible harms where waiting for empirical feedback could be too late.

**Potential follow-up:** Add Policy Umwelt/WPR as a comparative concept in the literature review or discussion, especially for the transition from discourse formation to governance implications. Consider a LaclauGPT-derived corpus measure or researcher memo for `problem representation → privileged interventions → excluded/silent alternatives`, but keep this as a human-validated corpus-level interpretation rather than automatic classification. The three manifesto cases also provide useful benchmark material for testing whether the pipeline can distinguish similar policy instruments justified through different ideological articulations.

### Stephen Cave & Kanta Dihal (eds.) — *Imagining AI: How the World Sees Intelligent Machines* (2023)

**Bibliographic note:** Oxford University Press. ISBN `978-0-19-286536-6`. DOI: `10.1093/oso/9780192865366.001.0001`. Edited collection arising from the Global AI Narratives project at the Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence, University of Cambridge.

**Core argument / research question:** The collection asks how intelligent machines are imagined across different cultures and regions, and how those historically specific narratives shape the meanings, hopes, fears, policies, and technological trajectories associated with AI. Cave and Dihal argue that AI was a cultural phenomenon before it was a technological category and that contemporary AI remains entangled with long-standing myths and fantasies. The volume therefore rejects the assumption that there is a single universal “AI imaginary.” Instead, AI is continually reinterpreted through language, history, ideology, religion, political economy, colonial experience, literature, popular culture, and national development projects.

**Methods / material:** This is an interdisciplinary edited collection rather than a single-method empirical study. It grew from twenty Global AI Narratives workshops conducted from 2018 to 2021 with collaborators across six continents. Chapters analyze heterogeneous material including myths, literature, science fiction, comics, visual art, film, government propaganda, policy documents, technological discourse, and ethnographic observations. The book is organized geographically across Europe; the Americas and Pacific; Africa, the Middle East and South Asia; and East and South East Asia. The editors explicitly refuse a strict fiction/non-fiction divide because both are treated as components of sociotechnical imaginaries.

**Key findings or claims:**

- **AI is culturally constituted as well as technically produced.** The category “artificial intelligence” rests on older fantasies of intelligent machines, and contemporary technological development interacts continuously with mythology and narrative. Academia, industry, media, culture, and policy form mutually influential sites of AI production.
- **AI narratives form sociotechnical imaginaries.** Fictional and non-fictional representations jointly influence public perception and the direction of technological development. Narratives therefore matter to funding, engineering goals, product design, adoption, regulation, and governance rather than merely reflecting technological change after the fact.
- **The signifier “AI” travels unevenly across languages.** The cross-cultural terminology chapter traces “artificial” and “intelligence” across Germanic, Romance, Slavonic, Japanese, and Chinese contexts. Terms carry different histories and value connotations, so translation does not simply reproduce an invariant technological concept. This is especially important for discourse analysis because apparently equivalent labels can occupy different semantic and ideological positions.
- **The American AI imaginary is structured by an unstable utopia/dystopia relation.** US narratives repeatedly imagine AI as a route to mastery over nature, mortality, scarcity, and human limitation, while the same imagined superhuman power generates fears of loss of control, enslavement, or extinction. The chapter connects these narratives to American histories of technological supremacy, settler colonialism, racial hierarchy, and domination. Utopian technological mastery and catastrophic x-risk can therefore be interpreted as two sides of a shared imaginary rather than wholly independent discourses.
- **Global AI imaginaries cannot be reduced to the American loss-of-control template.** Japanese narratives considered in the volume emphasize animacy, emotion, companionship, and human-machine partnership; contemporary Chinese cases draw on different philosophical traditions and histories; Singaporean narratives often emphasize coexistence and mutual dependence rather than annihilation or enslavement. These examples complicate claims that existential-risk narratives are culturally universal.
- **Developmentalism strongly shapes AI policy discourse.** So Young Kim shows how South Korean AI policy after AlphaGo reproduces older developmental-state structures: state-led planning, technology as an engine of economic growth, global catch-up rhetoric, strategic investment, and industrial competitiveness. Even AI ethics policy is partly articulated through industrial-development priorities. The case demonstrates how a globally circulating signifier such as AI can be inserted into an older national hegemonic project.
- **AI can function as a vehicle of colonial and extractive power.** Abeba Birhane describes algorithmic colonialism as domination exercised through digital ecosystems, infrastructure, data extraction, categorization, and technology monopolies rather than direct territorial rule. Western technology firms can present expansion into Africa through narratives of connection, financial inclusion, development, modernization, and technological solutions while simultaneously concentrating epistemic and infrastructural authority. Birhane stresses that this is not an argument against AI as such, but against imposed business models, universalizing frameworks, and power relations that suppress local knowledge and locally governed alternatives.
- **Decolonial and Indigenous perspectives generate alternative imaginaries rather than merely criticizing Western AI.** Chapters on Afrofuturism in Brazil, Indigenous AI, Maoli Intelligence, African and postcolonial perspectives, and MENA histories foreground counter-narratives, data sovereignty, alternative understandings of intelligence and personhood, resistance to algorithmic racism, and culturally situated technological futures.
- **National and regional imaginaries remain internally heterogeneous.** The editors caution against treating cultures as sealed units. Narratives travel through colonialism, migration, globalization, media, political ideology, and technological exchange. The comparative unit should therefore be treated as historically relational rather than as an essential national “culture.”

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** The volume offers an unusually strong bridge between **sociotechnical imaginaries and Laclaudian discourse analysis**. It supports treating `AI`, `intelligence`, `progress`, `development`, `control`, `risk`, `humanity`, and related terms as historically variable signifiers rather than transparent labels for fixed objects. The cross-linguistic chapter is particularly useful for LLM Structuralism because it demonstrates that semantic relations and evaluative connotations change across linguistic systems. The book also offers numerous candidate articulations for comparative coding: `AI → progress → development → national competitiveness`; `AI → superintelligence → loss of control → extinction`; `AI → infrastructure → extraction → colonialism`; and alternative chains around partnership, care, sovereignty, coexistence, and situated knowledge.

A second important contribution is the idea that **ideological formations can share deep narrative grammar while disagreeing politically**. In the American case, techno-utopian mastery and existential-risk fear both depend on imagining extraordinarily powerful machines and a hierarchy structured by intelligence and control. This suggests an AI26 research question: accelerationist and doomer discourse may sometimes constitute antagonistic positions inside a broader shared American/Californian imaginary rather than completely separate ideological universes.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_1_PAPER.md`:** Very high. AI26 studies ideological contestation over AI across elite, parliamentary/policy, and grassroots arenas. *Imagining AI* supplies strong comparative evidence that the object being contested is itself culturally and historically constructed. It therefore strengthens the rationale for treating AI/AGI as a potentially floating or tendentially empty signifier whose meaning is produced through articulation rather than assumed in advance. The book also warns against deriving a supposedly global codebook exclusively from US elite discourse: x-risk, accelerationism, Critical AI, developmentalism, decolonial critique, partnership/coexistence imaginaries, Indigenous perspectives, and other formations may overlap differently across countries and languages.

For the empirical design, the volume suggests analyzing both **transnational formations and nationally sedimented imaginaries**. The same signifier can travel globally while becoming articulated to different histories: US frontier/mastery narratives, South Korean developmental-state catch-up, African anti-colonial and infrastructural concerns, Japanese human-machine relationality, or Chinese philosophical and political traditions. This is directly compatible with LaclauGPT’s relational approach because such patterns can be tested as recurring chains, oppositions, nodal points, and frontiers rather than encoded as immutable cultural labels.

**Limitations / cautions:** The volume is deliberately wide-ranging and heterogeneous. Chapters use different disciplines, materials, scales, and evidentiary standards, so their findings should not be treated as one standardized comparative dataset. Coverage is also uneven: the editors explicitly acknowledge stronger representation of some regions, especially Europe and China, and thinner coverage of places such as Sub-Saharan Africa and India. The geographical organization can tempt readers to essentialize national or regional cultures, although the editors themselves repeatedly caution against this. Much of the empirical material predates the post-2022 generative-AI boom, so the book is best used as a genealogy and comparative framework for AI26, not as a description of the current ideological field.

**Potential follow-up:** Use the book to add a cross-cultural/sociotechnical-imaginary layer to AI26 without turning national identity into a fixed classifier. Candidate human-validated corpus questions include: Which older national or cultural narratives are being re-articulated around generative AI and AGI? Do accelerationist and doomer texts share a deeper loss-of-control/mastery grammar? How do `AI`, `intelligence`, `progress`, `risk`, and `development` shift semantically across languages? Does parliamentary AI discourse reproduce national developmental or geopolitical traditions while elite AI discourse remains more transnational? Birhane’s chapter is also an obvious bridge to future Kenya/Africa work, particularly for examining how AI-development discourse articulates infrastructure, dependency, extraction, sovereignty, and locally governed alternatives.

### Philip Leifeld — *Discourse Network Analysis: Policy Debates as Dynamic Networks* (2017)

**Bibliographic note:** Chapter 12/25 in *The Oxford Handbook of Political Networks*, pp. 301–326. DOI: `10.1093/oxfordhb/9780190228217.013.25`. Two uploaded PDFs are versions of the same chapter; they are summarized once here.

**Core argument / research question:** Political discourse should be treated as a **dynamic network phenomenon** because actors make public policy claims in relation to one another, agree or disagree over concepts, and change positions and coalition relations through time. Discourse Network Analysis (DNA) combines qualitative, category-based content analysis with descriptive and inferential network analysis so that the structure and evolution of policy debates can be studied empirically.

**Methods / material:** The basic observation is a coded statement linking an **actor**, a **concept**, a positive/negative **stance**, and a **time stamp**. From these structured statements DNA derives actor–concept affiliation networks, actor congruence networks, actor conflict networks, concept congruence networks, time-window networks, and temporally attenuated networks. The chapter uses German pension-politics data as a major example and discusses normalization, clustering, coalition dynamics, polarization, brokerage, and inferential network models.

**Key findings or claims:**

- Topic co-occurrence is insufficient for political discourse analysis. Two actors can invoke the same concept while taking opposite positions, so stance has to be represented explicitly.
- Actor–concept bipartite networks preserve the original coded structure; projected congruence and conflict networks make coalitions and oppositions more visible.
- DNA can identify advocacy/discourse coalitions, coalition switching, cohesion, brokerage, polarization, consensus formation, and changes in issue structure over time.
- Temporal windows and attenuation functions allow recent discursive interaction to count more strongly than distant agreement or disagreement, providing a processual view rather than a static cross-section.
- In the pension case, successive networks show a dominant corporatist coalition giving way to bipolarization, coalition switching, erosion of the old coalition, and institutionalization of a privatization-oriented coalition before reform.
- Concept networks can be clustered to reveal coherent bundles of policy positions or frames, but interpretation still requires attention to actor groups and coding choices.
- DNA is extensible: the statement schema can be modified for political claims analysis or narrative-policy analysis by adding fields such as addressee, hero, villain, tone, or location.
- Leifeld identifies semi-automatic statement recognition as an important frontier because inferential DNA needs large volumes of reliably coded statements.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** `actor → concept → stance → time` is an excellent common intermediate representation for AI26. It provides a structural layer underneath theory-specific interpretation. Actor congruence can operationalize candidate discourse coalitions; conflict networks can identify structured opposition; concept networks can expose recurrent bundles of positions; time-aware networks can track coalition formation and fragmentation. Most importantly, DNA can supply independent relational evidence before an LLM proposes Laclaudian equivalence, antagonism, nodal points, or ideological formations.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_1_PAPER.md`:** Very high methodologically. The paper currently emphasizes relational articulation and warns that co-occurrence, frequency, or sentiment cannot by themselves establish political relations. DNA offers a complementary empirical representation that respects this relational principle while being less theory-specific than Laclaudian coding. For AI26 it can support comparison across elite, grassroots, and parliamentary arenas and allow provisional accelerationist, x-risk, Critical AI, anti-AI, or techno-optimist formations to emerge as network patterns rather than fixed document labels.

**Limitations / cautions:** DNA is only as valid as its statement coding and concept normalization. Projected networks lose information relative to the original bipartite statement structure, and results can depend strongly on normalization, thresholds, temporal windows, and concept granularity. Network communities are not automatically ideologies, political subjects, or hegemonic blocs. Congruence is not Laclaudian equivalence; conflict is not necessarily antagonism. The method was developed for policy debates and may need careful adaptation for social-media discourse where speakers quote, mock, repost, or ambiguously invoke claims.

**Potential follow-up:** Implement a native Python DNA layer in `LaclauGPT-Data-Analysis`, centered on stable actor, concept, stance, evidence, and time fields. Export actor–concept, congruence, conflict, and temporal networks for `LaclauGPT-Data-Visualization`. Use DNA structures as evidence for, not substitutes for, human-validated interpretive analysis. Validate semi-automatic LLM statement extraction against a manually coded AI26 sample.

### Robert D. Benford & David A. Snow — *Framing Processes and Social Movements: An Overview and Assessment* (2000)

**Bibliographic note:** *Annual Review of Sociology* 26: 611–639.

**Core argument / research question:** The article reviews the rapidly expanding literature on collective-action framing and asks whether it forms a coherent perspective capable of explaining important dimensions of social-movement dynamics. Framing is treated as active, processual, agentic, and contentious **meaning construction**, not merely as a static topic or message category.

**Methods / material:** Synthetic review of theoretical and empirical social-movement research. The authors organize the literature around the conceptual features of collective-action frames, core framing tasks, discursive and strategic framing processes, contextual conditions, frame resonance, diffusion, identities, and movement outcomes.

**Key findings or claims:**

- Collective-action frames are action-oriented sets of meanings that simplify and condense aspects of the world in ways intended to mobilize supporters, gain bystander support, and demobilize opponents.
- The article distinguishes framing from ideology. Ideology is generally broader, more coherent, and durable; collective-action frames can amplify, extend, transform, or counter elements of ideologies.
- Three core framing tasks are central: **diagnostic framing** identifies a problem and often attributes responsibility; **prognostic framing** proposes solutions and strategies; **motivational framing** supplies a rationale or call to action.
- Diagnostic framing can include adversarial and boundary work that constructs protagonists and antagonists. Cognitive blame and affective anger need not target the same actor.
- Prognostic framing is constrained by diagnosis: how a problem is defined narrows the range of solutions that appear reasonable. It also develops in interaction with opponents and counterframes.
- Frames vary in flexibility, inclusivity, scope, and influence. Some broad, adaptable frames can become **master frames** that travel across movements.
- Strategic frame-alignment processes include **bridging**, **amplification**, **extension**, and **transformation**.
- Frames are continuously produced and reworked in interaction; the authors caution against treating them as fixed textual objects detached from ongoing movement activity.
- Framing can affect mobilization and outcomes, but the causal evidence is uneven and context-dependent.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** The diagnostic–prognostic–motivational triad is highly operationalizable for AI26 and complements DNA well. It can distinguish, for example, whether AI is framed as a labour threat, civilizational risk, productivity engine, colonial infrastructure, or emancipatory technology; who or what is blamed; what remedy follows; and why action is demanded. Boundary/adversarial framing provides a useful empirical precursor to stronger claims about antagonistic frontiers. Frame bridging and master frames may help explain how previously distinct AI grievances become connected.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_1_PAPER.md`:** High, especially for the grassroots-mobilization arena. The article supplies a less Laclau-specific language for studying problem construction, demands, opposition, identity, and mobilization. It also reinforces the current paper’s distinction between ideology and more situated discursive formations: frame analysis can operate at statement/document level while broader ideological formation remains a corpus-level interpretation.

**Limitations / cautions:** The review is centered on social movements, so not every concept transfers directly to corporate, scientific, or parliamentary AI discourse. Frame frequency does not establish resonance or effectiveness. The framework can tempt analysts to code neat diagnostic/prognostic categories where texts are ambiguous or multi-vocal. The article itself emphasizes process and interaction, so a purely document-classification implementation would miss part of the theory.

**Potential follow-up:** Add structured AI26 fields for diagnosis/problem, causal attribution/blame, prognosis/remedy, motivation, protagonists/antagonists, and counterframing. Where possible, compare frame structures across DNA coalitions and through time rather than treating “frame” as a single categorical label.

### Erving Goffman — *Frame Analysis: An Essay on the Organization of Experience* (1974)

**Bibliographic note:** Originally published by Harper & Row; uploaded edition is the 1986 Northeastern University Press reprint. The uploaded PDF is image-based, so this summary is deliberately limited to material visible in the scanned introduction and opening chapter rather than pretending to reconstruct every part of the book.

**Core argument / research question:** Goffman asks how people organize experience so that they can answer the practical question of **“What is it that’s going on here?”** His aim is to isolate basic frameworks of understanding available in society for making sense of events and to study the vulnerabilities, transformations, and complications of those frameworks.

**Methods / material:** Conceptual sociology illustrated through many heterogeneous examples, anecdotes, press stories, ordinary interaction, performances, mistakes, games, staged events, and other cases. Goffman explicitly presents the work as an exploratory analysis using strips of activity rather than a formal quantitative method.

**Key findings or claims:**

- A **frame** is a scheme of interpretation that renders otherwise meaningless aspects of a scene meaningful.
- Goffman begins with **primary frameworks**, broad ways of making events intelligible without requiring reference to a prior interpretation.
- He distinguishes **natural frameworks**, which interpret events as unguided physical happenings, from **social frameworks**, which interpret events as guided doings involving will, aim, standards, and agency.
- Social activity is usually understood through background frameworks that allow observers and participants to recognize what kind of event is taking place and what expectations apply.
- Frame analysis is concerned with how experience is organized, transformed, keyed, fabricated, misunderstood, or otherwise rendered vulnerable to shifts in interpretation.
- The unit of inquiry is not simply linguistic content. It is organized experience and the interpretive framework through which activity becomes intelligible.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** Goffman provides the deep conceptual background for later frame analysis but should not be collapsed into Entman or Snow/Benford coding. His most useful lesson for LaclauGPT is that a frame is not merely a topic label; it is an interpretive organization that makes an event recognizable as a particular kind of event. For AI26 this encourages analysis of how the same occurrence, such as a model release, worker displacement, data-centre construction, or safety incident, is rendered as innovation, threat, exploitation, competition, governance failure, or progress.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_1_PAPER.md`:** Moderate but conceptually useful. Goffman can strengthen the non-Laclaudian framing layer and help prevent computational “frame detection” from degenerating into topic classification. His work also fits the paper’s wider concern with how social meaning is produced through relational interpretation rather than transparently read off from events themselves.

**Limitations / cautions:** Goffman is not providing a political ideology theory or a ready-made computational codebook. The book’s scope is much broader than political framing, and its concepts should not be reduced to the diagnostic/prognostic schema of social-movement research. Because the uploaded scan has no machine-readable text, this entry is intentionally conservative and should be expanded only after a readable edition or targeted manual review of later chapters.

**Potential follow-up:** Treat Goffman as conceptual grounding, while using Entman and Benford/Snow for operational coding. If frame analysis becomes a major methodological contribution, obtain a text-readable copy and expand the source note around keying, fabrication, frame breaks, and the organization of interaction.

### Brigitte Le Roux & Henry Rouanet — *Multiple Correspondence Analysis* (2010/2011)

**Bibliographic note:** Sage, Quantitative Applications in the Social Sciences. The uploaded Sage Research Methods edition lists publication date 2011 and DOI `10.4135/9781412993906`.

**Core argument / research question:** The book presents Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) as a central method of **Geometric Data Analysis (GDA)** for full-scale social-science research. Its guiding idea is that categorical data can be represented geometrically as clouds of individuals and categories, allowing researchers to examine relational structure without forcing a dependent-variable model onto the data.

**Methods / material:** Nontechnical but methodologically precise introduction to MCA using a taste dataset inspired by Bourdieu. Individuals × categorical-variables tables are transformed into geometric spaces using linear algebra. The book explains principal axes, inertia, clouds of points, contributions, active and supplementary elements, structured data analysis, and inductive statistical tools.

**Key findings or claims:**

- GDA begins with **geometric modeling**: individuals and categories become points in a multidimensional space whose relational structure is the object of analysis.
- The methodological priority is descriptive: the model should follow the data rather than the reverse. Statistical explanation must be supplied by substantive theory, not by the mathematics alone.
- **Contributions** identify which categories most strongly construct an axis and are therefore a primary aid to interpretation.
- **Active variables** construct the space; **supplementary variables/individuals** can be projected afterward to enrich interpretation without determining the axes.
- MCA is particularly suited to relational field analysis because it examines systems of categorical differences rather than isolated bivariate effects.
- The authors explicitly connect MCA to Bourdieu, who preferred correspondence methods because they “think in relations” and align with his concept of field.
- Bourdieu’s move from traditional statistics to correspondence analysis and then MCA reflected dissatisfaction with analyses that isolate independent effects and obscure the full system of relations.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** MCA offers a second structural representation of AI26 that is fundamentally different from networks. DNA asks who agrees with whom about what; MCA asks what **relational space of positions** is generated by categorical differences. Actor type, arena, claims, frame components, governance preferences, ownership/control positions, risk orientations, and other reviewed categorical variables can construct a field without hard-coding an accel/doomer/critical axis. Existing ideological labels can then be projected as supplementary variables.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_1_PAPER.md`:** High for the experimental AI26 methodology, even if it is not yet part of the paper. MCA operationalizes relational thinking in a way compatible with the paper’s refusal of fixed ideological categories. It can provide an independent geometric map against which LaclauGPT’s proposed ideological formations, signifiers, and coalitions can be compared.

**Limitations / cautions:** MCA does not explain social mechanisms by itself, and axes do not come with intrinsic substantive meanings. Variable selection can strongly shape the space; rare categories can distort interpretation; and an attractive two-dimensional plot can hide important higher dimensions. LLM-generated categories should not be fed indiscriminately into MCA. Active versus supplementary status must be theoretically justified.

**Potential follow-up:** Build actor-level and statement/document-level AI26 MCA pipelines using carefully selected active variables, project provisional ideological labels as supplementary variables, report category contributions and quality-of-representation measures, and compare the resulting field to DNA coalition structure.

### Pierre Bourdieu — *Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste* (1979/1984)

**Bibliographic note:** Originally published in French as *La Distinction* (1979); English translation by Richard Nice, Harvard University Press, 1984.

**Core argument / research question:** Bourdieu argues that taste is socially structured rather than an autonomous expression of individual preference. Cultural judgements classify objects and simultaneously **classify the classifier**: patterns of taste, lifestyle, political opinion, and practice express positions in social space generated by unequal distributions and compositions of capital. Habitus transforms social conditions into durable dispositions, making socially structured practices appear natural or personal.

**Methods / material:** Large-scale empirical sociology combining surveys, interviews, statistics, correspondence analysis, cultural examples, and relational interpretation. The book maps social positions, lifestyles, cultural practices, political opinions, and class fractions, with special attention to the volume and composition of economic and cultural capital.

**Key findings or claims:**

- Social space is structured relationally. Bourdieu highlights dimensions including **volume of capital**, **composition of capital**, and trajectory/change over time.
- Capital is field-specific in its efficacy: resources only function as capital through the rules and relations of a particular field.
- **Habitus** is a system of dispositions produced by social conditions that generates practices adjusted to those conditions without requiring conscious calculation.
- Taste produces and reproduces distinctions. Preferences gain social meaning from their position within systems of oppositions and homologous relations.
- Spaces of lifestyle and cultural consumption are structured in ways homologous to objective social-space oppositions, especially relations between economic and cultural capital.
- The “taste of necessity” and “taste of luxury/liberty” show how materially different conditions become aestheticized and misrecognized as natural preference.
- Political position-taking is related to social position. Bourdieu treats political space as a transformed or skewed relation to social space rather than a simple one-dimensional opinion scale.
- Classification itself is a site of struggle: social groups struggle not only over resources but also over legitimate categories of perception and representation.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** `field`, `position`, `position-taking`, `capital`, `habitus`, `social space`, `homology`, and `classification struggle` are especially useful. For AI26, Bourdieu suggests distinguishing **what actors say** from **where actors are positioned** institutionally and materially. AI labs, founders, academics, safety organizations, Critical AI researchers, unions, politicians, artists, and activists occupy different positions and possess different mixtures of economic, institutional, scientific, media, and symbolic capital.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_1_PAPER.md`:** Potentially very high as an experimental complement to Laclau. Laclau is strong on articulation and hegemonic construction but comparatively weak on the structured social positions from which actors speak. Bourdieu/MCA can map the field of AI discourse empirically, while LaclauGPT can analyze how actors occupying those positions articulate AI, demands, identities, and futures. This may help distinguish **discourse coalition** from **field position** rather than assuming they are the same thing.

**Limitations / cautions:** *Distinction* is based primarily on twentieth-century French class and cultural data; its substantive findings should not be transplanted directly to contemporary global AI politics. Bourdieu’s theory can also tempt analysts to explain discourse too directly from social position. AI26 should test relations between field positions and position-taking rather than assume them. Institutional and symbolic capital in an AI field will need study-specific operationalization.

**Potential follow-up:** Define an AI26 actor-field schema with institution type and selected forms of capital/status as supplementary metadata; use MCA to construct relational spaces from discourse variables; then test whether DNA coalitions and frame patterns align with, cut across, or bridge field positions.

### Hans-Georg Moeller — *Luhmann Explained: From Souls to Systems* (2006)

**Bibliographic note:** Open Court, 2006. Introductory exposition of Niklas Luhmann’s social systems theory, including discussion of world society, mass media, protest, ethics, and translations of selected Luhmann texts.

**Core argument / research question:** Moeller presents Luhmann as offering a radically nonhumanist, cybernetic description of modern society. Society is not an aggregate of human beings or intentions but a system of **communications** differentiated into autonomous function systems. The book’s aim is pedagogical: to make Luhmann’s difficult theory intelligible while showing why it offers an alternative to humanist self-descriptions of politics and society.

**Methods / material:** Conceptual exposition and interpretation of Luhmann’s systems theory, with examples from politics, economy, law, science, mass media, globalization, ecological communication, protest, and ethics.

**Key findings or claims:**

- Autopoietic systems reproduce themselves through their own operations: communications continue through communications, psychic operations through psychic operations, biological operations through biological operations.
- **Operational closure** does not mean isolation. Systems respond to environmental irritations according to their own structures but do not directly import outside operations.
- Modern society is **functionally differentiated** into systems such as politics, law, economy, science, religion, and mass media, each reproducing itself through its own distinctions and code.
- Functional differentiation makes modern society a **world society**: most function systems transcend territorial borders even while politics remains partly segmented into states.
- No single function system occupies a sovereign position from which society as a whole can be centrally steered.
- Mass media are treated as an increasingly consequential function system whose reality-production follows its own operational logic.
- Protest and activism operate as communication inside differentiated society. Their intended effects cannot simply be assumed to translate into corresponding changes in other systems.
- Moeller emphasizes Luhmann’s suspicion of humanist semantics such as democracy, liberty, and human rights when these are treated as transparent descriptions of how society actually functions.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** `communication`, `system/environment`, `autopoiesis`, `operational closure`, `functional differentiation`, `binary code`, `observation`, `second-order observation`, `resonance`, and `world society`. For AI26, Luhmann offers a way to analyze how “AI” is differently processed by politics, science, economy, law, journalism, activism, and technology organizations without assuming one discourse controls the others.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_1_PAPER.md`:** Indirect but potentially powerful as a future extension. The paper currently compares arenas; Luhmann suggests treating those arenas as partly distinct communicative systems or organizational contexts with different selection logics. This could explain why identical AI claims change meaning or consequences when translated from research to markets, media, parliament, law, or protest. It also complements Laclau’s focus on hegemonic articulation with a theory skeptical of unified societal steering.

**Limitations / cautions:** Moeller is an interpreter of Luhmann, not Luhmann himself, and deliberately foregrounds the radical/anti-humanist reading. Systems theory is conceptually demanding and can become too totalizing if every empirical observation is simply redescribed as system reproduction. Its relation to agency, power, inequality, and material infrastructures needs supplementation. It should not be mixed casually into the current paper without a clear analytical payoff.

**Potential follow-up:** Keep Luhmann outside the current core paper for now, but test an AI26 “functional translation” view: how the same AI issue is coded differently by science, markets, politics, law, media, and protest. If useful, use systems theory at the corpus/arena level rather than adding Luhmann terms to every document annotation.

### Hans-Georg Moeller — *The Radical Luhmann* (2012)

**Bibliographic note:** Columbia University Press, 2012.

**Core argument / research question:** Moeller argues that the genuinely radical quality of Luhmann’s social systems theory is often underestimated. Luhmann rejects humanism, central social steering, teleological philosophy, simple realism/constructivism oppositions, and normative political self-descriptions, replacing them with a theory of contingent, self-reproducing systems and observation.

**Methods / material:** Philosophical reconstruction of Luhmann organized around themes including antihumanism, contingency, mind/body, evolution, constructivism, and democracy. Moeller reads Luhmann against major traditions of Western philosophy and political theory rather than presenting an empirical case study.

**Key findings or claims:**

- Luhmann’s sociology “de-humanizes” society analytically: biological bodies, psychic systems, and communication systems are operationally distinct and cannot be collapsed into a unified human subject that serves as society’s basic element.
- Society consists of communications and function systems, not human agents considered as an integrated systemic unit.
- Humanist political theory assumes that society can be steered through politics, but under functional differentiation there is no central agency capable of steering society as a whole.
- Political systems can perturb or irritate other systems, but steering is ultimately system-specific self-steering.
- Moeller treats Luhmann’s theory as radical precisely because it challenges dominant social self-descriptions such as democracy, freedom, solidarity, and human-centered agency rather than merely demanding their fuller realization.
- The book presents Luhmannian constructivism as a theory of observation and difference rather than as a denial of reality.
- Contingency replaces necessity and teleology: modern society cannot be adequately explained through a singular developmental goal or privileged foundational standpoint.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** The most useful contribution for AI26 is a sharp warning against assuming that political discourse maps directly onto societal control. Claims such as “government must control AI,” “markets will direct AI,” or “labs are steering civilization” can be analyzed as self-descriptions and steering imaginaries rather than taken as descriptions of actual system-wide control. The system/environment distinction also invites comparison with discourse boundaries and arena-specific meaning production.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_1_PAPER.md`:** More theoretical than methodological. It could eventually provide a productive counterpoint to Laclau: Laclau asks how projects construct a hegemonic representation of the social whole, while Luhmann questions whether society has a single center or steering subject at all. That tension could become valuable in later theoretical work on AI governance and “who controls AI,” but it would broaden the current paper considerably.

**Limitations / cautions:** Moeller pushes a particularly radical reading of Luhmann and is explicitly skeptical of humanist and normative political vocabularies. This is useful analytically but should not be mistaken for a neutral empirical conclusion. The framework can underplay domination, unequal resources, organized power, and the strategic agency of actors. For AI26 it is better suited to comparative system-level interpretation than to replacing DNA, framing, MCA, or discourse analysis.

**Potential follow-up:** Use this source when developing a later theory of **AI governance as competing steering imaginaries** across function systems. Compare Luhmannian polycentric/non-steerable society with Laclaudian hegemonic projects that symbolically claim to represent and govern the whole.

### Hannah Richter — *The Politics of Orientation: Deleuze Meets Luhmann* (2023)

**Bibliographic note:** State University of New York Press, 2023. The book explicitly reads Deleuze and Luhmann together and culminates in an analysis of post-truth populism as a politics of orientation.

**Core argument / research question:** Richter argues that contemporary democratic politics increasingly functions less by demonstrating effective steering of society and more by providing **orientation**: complexity-reducing frameworks through which subjects make sense of themselves, the world, and their position within it. By combining Deleuze and Luhmann, she interprets post-truth populism not simply as a breakdown of rational democracy but as a particularly effective form of this broader politics of orientation under conditions of globalized, capitalist, digitalized, functionally differentiated society.

**Methods / material:** Theoretical synthesis of Luhmann’s systems theory and Deleuzian philosophy, developed through chapters on sense, autopoiesis, time, political events, orientation, and post-truth populism. The final analysis engages existing accounts by Wendy Brown, Chantal Mouffe, and others.

**Key findings or claims:**

- Modern politics faces declining capacity to steer an increasingly complex, differentiated society while still needing to present itself as a collective steering authority.
- **Orientation** is a form of complexity reduction: politics supplies diagnoses, values, narratives, explanatory frameworks, and identity/world pictures that help subjects locate themselves in an opaque environment.
- Contemporary political support can therefore attach to world- and identity-making visions even when detailed programs and concrete steering capacities are weak.
- Post-truth populism is interpreted as an effective orientation mechanism because drastic negation, simple distinctions, and control of intransparency reduce complexity and offer certainty.
- Richter does not treat post-truth populism as entirely discontinuous with modern democracy. It is an adaptation of political autopoiesis under changed social conditions.
- A key Deleuzian modification of Luhmann is that codes are treated as historically produced, relational, flexible, and path-dependent rather than fully fixed. Codes can overlap, conflict, and change through use.
- Material objects, bodies, spaces, signs, and institutions all participate in the structures that orient sense, preventing orientation from becoming purely linguistic.
- The book explicitly compares its framework to Mouffe’s theory of populism and argues that the politics-of-orientation perspective places stronger explanatory weight on complexity reduction and socio-historical conditions.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** `orientation`, `complexity reduction`, `intransparency`, `sense-making`, `coding`, `self/world-making`, and `political steering` are potentially valuable higher-level concepts. AI discourse is saturated with orientation problems: uncertainty about technological trajectories, employment, security, expertise, control, and human identity. Accelerationist, x-risk, Critical AI, and anti-AI discourses can each be studied as ways of making an opaque technological future intelligible.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_1_PAPER.md`:** Conceptually intriguing but not required for the current paper. Richter offers a bridge between Luhmann and Mouffe and a strong alternative lens on the same empirical terrain: instead of asking only how AI is hegemonically articulated, one can ask what forms of **orientation under uncertainty** competing AI discourses provide. This is especially promising for AI26 because many highly visible AI positions are future-oriented and simplify radical uncertainty into compelling narratives of abundance, extinction, exploitation, sovereignty, or control.

**Limitations / cautions:** The book is a philosophical synthesis rather than a computational methodology, and its post-truth-populism discussion concerns a specific political domain that should not be transferred wholesale to AI discourse. “Orientation” is broad and could become a catch-all concept unless operationalized carefully. The work’s synthesis of Deleuze and Luhmann is itself interpretive and should not be treated as a consensus reading of either thinker.

**Potential follow-up:** Keep this as an experimental theory layer for later AI26 analysis. A feasible operational question is: `uncertainty/problem → complexity-reducing distinction → orientation narrative → authorized actor/action → excluded alternatives`. Compare this to frame analysis and sociotechnical imaginaries before deciding whether “orientation” adds enough distinct explanatory value to enter the core methodology.

### Ma Hanlin — *From Leo XIV's Encyclical to Plural Technological Democracy: On the AI Governance Triangle* (2026)

**Bibliographic note:** Preprint, version 1.0, 27 July 2026. DOI: `10.5281/zenodo.21627389`. The manuscript explicitly states that it had not yet undergone peer review.

**Core argument / research question:** Ma compares several AI-governance positions that rest on incompatible conceptions of technology, competition, domination, and the good. The paper proposes an **AI governance triangle** with three vertices: commitment to super-technology, opposition to horizontal/homogenizing competition, and opposition to technological domination. It then adds a structural non-domination framework based on the positions of those who design, finance, regulate, operate, and use technology.

**Methods / material:** Conceptual-comparative political theory. The paper reconstructs positions associated with Leo XIV's Catholic social teaching, Peter Thiel's anti-competitive technological politics, cyberlibertarian/effective accelerationism, defensive accelerationism, and Glen Weyl's pluralism. It uses close conceptual reconstruction rather than an empirical corpus.

**Key findings or claims:**

- Accelerationism treats breaking human limits and technological development as progress, while Catholic teaching interprets technological substitutes for transcendence much more negatively.
- Thiel and Catholic social teaching can both oppose homogenizing competition while doing so for incompatible reasons; shared policy coordinates therefore do not imply a shared ideology.
- e/acc is treated as a naturalized, affirmative super-technology position, while defensive accelerationism emerges as a reaction to the dangers of unconstrained acceleration yet may reproduce competitive pressures.
- The triangle is intentionally a coordinate system rather than a complete moral theory. It registers commitments before asking why they are held.
- A second framework analyzes where technological power sits across design, investment, regulation, operation, and use, allowing domination to be diagnosed without assuming that the state, firms, experts, or users are always the dominant party.
- Ma argues that pluralism is distinctive because it challenges the shared assumption that there ought to be a single supreme position of technological governance at all.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** The separation between **position/commitment** and **justificatory grammar** is especially useful. AI26 can observe that two actors support slowing, competition, open development, or centralized control without inferring that they belong to the same ideological formation. The triangle also suggests variables for MCA or framing: super-technology, competition, domination, concentration, pluralism, and governance position.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_1_PAPER.md`:** Useful primarily as a comparative source for accelerationist and governance discourse, not as evidence that accelerationism forms one coherent ideology. It supports the project's move away from permanent actor labels and toward statement-level relations. Its treatment of e/acc, d/acc, Thielian monopoly, Catholic restraint, and pluralism provides a compact map of overlapping but non-equivalent positions.

**Limitations / cautions:** This is a non-peer-reviewed 2026 preprint and a philosophical reconstruction rather than empirical analysis. The three chosen coordinates are themselves evaluative design choices, and the selected thinkers do not exhaust contemporary AI governance. The paper should therefore be used as a source of hypotheses and coding distinctions rather than as a definitive topology of the field.

**Potential follow-up:** Add the triangle's dimensions as optional supplementary variables in AI26 MCA and test whether empirical discourse actually organizes around them. Preserve `position`, `reason`, `actor`, and `institutional role` as distinct fields.

**Bibliographic note:** Doctoral dissertation, University of Helsinki, *Dissertationes Universitatis Helsingiensis* 63/2023. ISBN 978-951-51-9465-7 (online).

### Mario Angst, Neitah Noemi Müller & Viviane Walker — *Automated Extraction of Discourse Networks from Large Volumes of Media Data* (2025)

**Bibliographic note:** *Network Science* 13, e4, 1–23. DOI: `10.1017/nws.2025.4`. Open access.

**Core argument / research question:** The article asks whether discourse networks can be extracted at media scale without abandoning the actor–belief–stance structure central to Discourse Network Analysis. It presents an automated pipeline and tests when aggregated automated network representations become sufficiently valid for structural analysis.

**Methods / material:** Nearly two million newspaper articles covering twelve years of sustainable-transport discourse in Zürich, yielding more than one million paragraphs. The pipeline combines named-entity recognition and linking, supervised text classification informed by close reading, and LLM-based stance detection. Output is represented as a bipartite graph of organizations and beliefs connected by stance edges and evaluated against manually annotated data.

**Key findings or claims:**
- Large-scale DNA can be automated by decomposing extraction into actor identification, belief classification and stance detection rather than asking one model to infer an entire discourse network.
- The authors propose a **window validity hypothesis**: automated network data become more internally valid when observations are aggregated over sliding temporal windows.
- Redundancy and relative inertia of actors' stances can compensate for noisy document-level predictions.
- Windowed aggregation can recover basic network structure and higher-level descriptive network metrics substantially better than isolated statement classification.
- Automation does not eliminate qualitative work. High-quality manually coded test sets and close reading remain necessary, and the costs of automation should be justified by the research scale.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** `actor_belief_bipartite_graph`, `stance_edge`, `entity_linking`, `LLM_stance_detection`, `window_validity`, `stance_inertia`, `temporal_aggregation`, `gold_test_set`, and `human_validation`.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_1_PAPER.md`:** Extremely high for future DNA integration. It supplies a contemporary blueprint for moving from text/LLM outputs to longitudinal discourse networks while retaining explicit stance and provenance. The sliding-window result is also directly relevant to LaclauGPT memory: repeated observations over time can be more reliable than treating every LLM extraction as an independent fact.

**Limitations / cautions:** The empirical validation concerns one policy domain, one urban media environment and relatively persistent organizational positions. Aggregation can conceal genuine rapid position changes. The approach validates a DNA representation of actors, beliefs and stances, not Laclaudian equivalence, antagonism or hegemony.

**Potential follow-up:** When DNA returns after Phase 0, implement actor–concept–stance records as evidence-backed observations and derive windowed networks separately. Maintain manual benchmark sets and never translate co-occurrence or stance agreement automatically into Laclaudian relations.

### Harrison C. White — *Identity and Control: How Social Formations Emerge*, 2nd ed. (2008)

**Bibliographic note:** Princeton University Press, second edition. White develops a relational sociology in which identities, ties, stories, styles, disciplines, institutions, and regimes emerge through attempts to obtain social footing and control amid contingency.

**Core argument / research question:** White rejects fixed, self-contained actors as the natural starting point of sociology. An identity emerges relationally through attempts to secure footing in situations of uncertainty. “Control” does not primarily mean domination; it means attempts to stabilize orientation in relation to other identities. Identities and social formations emerge together through ties, stories, switching among network-domains (“netdoms”), embedding, decoupling, and recursive social processes.

**Methods / material:** A theoretical and synthetic work combining formal and descriptive social-network analysis, relational sociology, historical examples, organizational studies, linguistics, cultural analysis, and empirical network research. The book moves from identities and networks to stories, disciplines, styles, institutions, rhetorics, regimes of control, and collective action.

**Key findings or claims:**

- Identity is not restricted to an individual person. Firms, communities, crowds, roles, and other entities can function as identities when meaningful action is attributed to them.
- Identities gain footing through relations with other identities. Social order is an emergent and provisional outcome of overlapping control efforts.
- A **netdom** combines a network of relations with a domain of meanings/topics. Identities switch among netdoms, so an actor should not be assumed to have one context-independent identity.
- Networks and stories are inseparable: stories mark, interpret, justify, and reproduce ties.
- White uses structural equivalence, blockmodeling, tie types, coupling/decoupling, switching, styles, institutions, rhetorics, and regimes to describe emergent social formations at different scales.
- Cultural and social structure are intertwined. Meaning is generated in context, and communication is part of social structure/process rather than a separate layer added after network formation.
- White repeatedly engages Niklas Luhmann and treats recursive communication, functional subsystems, and self-reproduction as neighboring ideas while retaining a distinct network-relational vocabulary.
- Relations among identities can generate higher-order identities and formations, which can in turn constrain or reorganize lower-level relations.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** `identity`, `control`, `footing`, `netdom`, `network_story`, `switching`, `structural_equivalence`, `embedding`, `decoupling`, `discipline`, `style`, `rhetoric`, `regime_of_control`, `recursive_social_formation`.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_2_PAPER_DRAFT.md`:** White is one of the three primary theoretical sources proposed for the Phase 2 SNA layer. He provides a strong bridge between conventional SNA and discourse-oriented theory because he does not reduce networks to topology. Ties are inseparable from stories, identities are relational achievements rather than pre-given node attributes, and social formations emerge through changing configurations of relations and meanings.

White is especially useful for keeping the **actor model dynamic**. A social-media account, researcher, company, government agency, activist organization, or AI-linked hybrid should not automatically be treated as one stable actor across every arena. The same entity may occupy different netdoms and acquire different relational identities as topics, institutions, and communication contexts change.

The productive bridge to Laclau is not theoretical equivalence. White asks how identities and social formations emerge through networks, stories, and control; Laclau asks how political identities and hegemonic formations emerge through articulation, equivalence/difference, signification, and antagonism. Phase 2 can use White to theorize the social-relational layer while preserving Laclau as the primary theory of political meaning.

**Limitations / cautions:** White's vocabulary is dense and not a ready-made SNA codebook. Concepts such as netdom, style, discipline, rhetoric, and regime should not be converted automatically into graph metrics. His deliberate decentering of persons and consciousness should also not erase agency, institutional responsibility, or material differences among humans, organizations, and computational systems.

**Potential follow-up:** Use White selectively for the Phase 2 actor/identity ontology, multiplex network representation, contextual actor resolution, and bridges between stories and ties. Later empirical work can ask whether interaction communities correspond to discursive formations, whether switching across arenas changes actor position, and whether brokers between network domains translate political articulations.

### Elena Esposito — *Artificial Communication: How Algorithms Produce Social Intelligence* (2022)

**Bibliographic note:** MIT Press, Strong Ideas series. Esposito develops a Luhmann-inspired account of machine learning and algorithms as participants in **artificial communication**, shifting the analytical question away from whether machines possess human-like intelligence.

**Core argument / research question:** Esposito argues that comparing algorithmic performance with human intelligence is often misleading. The socially significant development is that algorithms can participate in communication without understanding in the human sense. The central question becomes how communication changes when computational systems can generate relevant, personalized, predictive, and actionable contributions to social communication.

**Methods / material:** Sociological theory supported by historical and contemporary examples. The book analyzes communication with algorithms through lists, visualization and digital text analysis, personalization, algorithmic memory, forgetting, and prediction. Niklas Luhmann's theory of communication is a major conceptual foundation.

**Key findings or claims:**

- Communicative capacity and intelligence should be analytically distinguished. An algorithm may function as a communication partner without being treated as a human-like mind.
- Machine-learning systems exploit differences and traces generated by users, recombining them into new information. Human contingency remains an indispensable input even when outputs are generated through opaque computational procedures.
- Successful communication does not require transparent access to another participant's cognition or intentions.
- Algorithmic personalization constructs individualized communication from population-scale behavioral traces rather than interpersonal knowledge.
- Algorithmic memory and prediction reorganize temporal relations by turning stored past behavior into expectations and interventions concerning possible futures.
- Prediction can work without causal explanation or human-style understanding, creating new capacities as well as feedback loops, opacity, overfitting, and self-fulfilling effects.
- Bias cannot be understood only as programmers' individual prejudice. Algorithms participate in already unequal and non-neutral communication and learn from data produced within those conditions.
- Problems surrounding algorithms are therefore communicative as well as technical, and different social domains require different forms of acceptable explanation, interpretation, ambiguity, responsibility, and control.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** `artificial_communication`, `algorithm_as_communication_partner`, `communication_without_understanding`, `social_intelligence`, `algorithmic_personalization`, `algorithmic_memory`, `prediction`, `feedback`, `communication_opacity`, `Luhmannian_communication`.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_2_PAPER_DRAFT.md`:** Esposito is the most direct theoretical bridge between the Phase 2 SNA/communication layer and AI26. AI is not only the topic being contested; algorithms, recommender systems, bots, and LLMs increasingly participate in infrastructures through which communication is generated, selected, personalized, summarized, amplified, and answered.

Her framework allows LaclauGPT to represent computational systems as **communication participants or mediators without equating them with human actors**. A human, organization, bot, recommender system, and LLM may all occupy roles in a communication graph, but they should not automatically be treated as ontologically or normatively equivalent.

Esposito also strengthens the methodological account of LLM-assisted analysis. LaclauGPT does not need to settle whether an LLM “really understands” discourse in order to study the communicative relation among researcher, corpus, prompt, model output, and human validation. The scientific question is whether this artificial communication yields reliable, inspectable, evidence-linked analytical operations.

**Limitations / cautions:** This is a sociological theory of algorithmic communication, not a validation study of LLM-based discourse analysis. “Communication partner” should not imply human-equivalent agency, intention, consciousness, responsibility, or political membership. The book also predates the mass adoption of post-2022 generative AI, so Phase 2 should extend its concepts cautiously.

**Potential follow-up:** Use Esposito to define typed communicator roles in Phase 2: human author, organization, platform, bot, recommender/ranking system, LLM-generated message, AI-assisted message, and researcher-model analytical interaction. Preserve provenance so LaclauGPT can distinguish who initiated, generated, selected, transformed, or amplified a communication event.

### Nathan Schneider — *Governable Spaces: Democratic Design for Online Life* (2024)

**Bibliographic note:** University of California Press, Luminos open-access monograph. DOI: `10.1525/luminos.181`.

**Core argument / research question:** Schneider argues that online communities have inherited an **implicit feudalism**: technical and cultural defaults concentrate authority in platform owners, administrators and “benevolent dictators.” Digital spaces are nevertheless governable institutions whose rules and architectures can be deliberately redesigned using democratic traditions and experimentation.

**Methods / material:** Interdisciplinary political and design analysis combining histories of governance, democratic theory, online-community cases, cooperative practice, feminist and abolitionist perspectives, and experiments in digital governance.

**Key findings or claims:**
- Governance is already embedded in platforms and communities even when designers describe their systems as neutral or merely technical.

- Technical design and political constitution are intertwined: permissions, moderation systems, ownership structures and protocols distribute power.
- Online spaces can draw on a much broader institutional repertoire, including cooperatives, councils, juries and other democratic mechanisms.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_1_PAPER.md`:** Mainly useful for the governance context of platforms and AI communities. It can help interpret grassroots/elite conflicts over who is authorized to govern AI and how apparently technical arrangements encode political choices.

**Limitations / cautions:** The book is normatively committed to democratic experimentation. Its governance vocabulary should not be treated as neutral classification, and the argument concerns online governance broadly rather than discourse analysis specifically.

**Potential follow-up:** Preserve governance proposals and institutional actors as extractable discourse objects, then analyze how competing formations articulate authority, participation, expertise and control instead of assigning a “democratic/undemocratic” score.

### Armin Nassehi — *Patterns: Theory of the Digital Society* (2024; German edition cited in the translation 2021)

**Bibliographic note:** Polity Press, translated by Mirko Wittwar. The English edition presents Nassehi's systems-theoretical sociology of digitalization. Rather than asking primarily what digital technology does to society, he asks what structural problem of modern society digitalization solves.

**Core argument / research question:** Nassehi argues that digitalization succeeds because modern society already has a structure that makes digital pattern recognition useful. Modern society is highly complex, functionally differentiated, difficult to observe as a whole, and full of recurring regularities that become visible through data. Digital technology is therefore not simply an external force imposed on society; it is a technological response to pre-existing problems of complexity, invisibility, coordination, and observation.

**Methods / material:** Social theory informed strongly by Niklas Luhmann, cybernetics, sociology of technology, and the history of quantification. The book develops its argument through the reference problem of digitalization, data, feedback, self-reference, duplication, traces, patterns, networks, coding/programming, learning technologies, the internet as mass media, privacy, and functional differentiation.

**Key findings or claims:**

- The guiding question is functional: **what problem does digitalization solve?** Nassehi locates the reference problem in the structure and complexity of modern society itself.
- Digitalization makes otherwise difficult-to-observe social regularities visible as patterns. The relevant object is not merely individual data points but recurring relations and distributions across many traces.
- Modern social research already practiced forms of pattern recognition before contemporary digital technologies. Digitalization radicalizes and automates this observational capacity.
- Data are not transparent copies of the world. Digital representations are selective duplications and observations that can become inputs for further observations, producing self-referential data worlds.
- Cybernetic feedback and digital communication create recursive relations among measurement, observation, decision, and subsequent behavior.
- Modern society is functionally differentiated. Political, economic, scientific, legal, media, religious, and other systems operate according to distinct communicative codes and programs while remaining mutually interdependent.
- Nassehi draws an analogy between the simple binary medium of digital technology and the simple codes through which functionally differentiated systems generate enormous complexity at the level of programs and forms.
- Digital technologies work partly because social structures are stable enough to generate patterns. Digitalization therefore reveals repetition and structural stability as well as change and fluidity.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** `pattern`, `pattern_recognition`, `reference_problem`, `digital_observation`, `data_as_observer`, `trace`, `feedback`, `self_reference`, `functional_differentiation`, `social_complexity`, `coding_and_programming`, `digitalization_of_communication`.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_2_PAPER_DRAFT.md`:** Nassehi is proposed as the third primary theoretical source for the Phase 2 SNA layer, alongside White and Esposito. He supplies a macro-sociological account of why digital network data are analytically possible in the first place. LaclauGPT does not encounter “society itself” in platform data; it encounters digital traces generated by communications and institutions, transformed into observable patterns through data infrastructures and analytical choices.

This is important because Phase 2 relies on network construction. A network is not simply found waiting inside a corpus. Nodes, edges, time windows, platform boundaries, entity-resolution rules, and projections are observational decisions that produce a patterned representation. Nassehi therefore supports methodological reflexivity around datafication and second-order observation.

He also gives the SNA layer a principled route to Luhmannian functional differentiation. Politics, economy, science, law, media, education, and other domains can be treated as differentiated communicative systems or contexts rather than as unrelated top-level ontologies.

**Limitations / cautions:** *Patterns* is macro-sociological theory, not an SNA manual or computational validation framework. Pattern recognition should not be equated with explanation, meaning, causality, ideology, or Laclaudian articulation. Used alone, systems theory can underplay conflict, domination, political economy, coloniality, labor, and unequal infrastructures; Phase 2 therefore needs Critical AI Studies and political sociology as counterweights.

**Potential follow-up:** Use Nassehi in the methodological-reflexivity section, the theory of digital traces, and social-system/context metadata. Later empirical work can test how discourse and communication patterns differ across functionally differentiated arenas without treating those patterns as self-explanatory.

### Hanna Wanselin, Kristina Danielsson & Susanne Wikman — *Analysing Multimodal Texts in Science—a Social Semiotic Perspective* (2022)

**Bibliographic note:** *Research in Science Education* 52:891–907. DOI: `10.1007/s11165-021-10027-5`. The paper develops a multimodal analytical framework from social semiotics, Systemic Functional Linguistics (SFL), Kress & van Leeuwen's visual grammar, and research on image–text relations.

**Core argument / research question:** The paper asks how multimodal texts can be analysed so that researchers can identify what content is expressed, which semiotic resources are used to express it, how those resources interact, and how authors position themselves through their semiotic choices. A central social-semiotic premise is that form and function are intertwined: a sign can be a word, image, gesture, or other meaning-making resource, and different modes have different affordances.

**Methods / material:** The authors combine SFL's textual, ideational, and interpersonal metafunctions with multimodal analysis. Their framework examines text organisation, choice and combination of modes, reading order, relative size and scale; processes, participants and circumstances; narrative versus conceptual visual functions; image–writing relations; positioning, speech roles, formality, and explicit or implicit values. Empirical examples are a student-produced ecology text and a digital teaching resource about the greenhouse effect.

**Key findings or claims:**

- Multimodal meaning cannot be recovered adequately from language alone. Image, writing, symbols, layout, scale, arrows, and other semiotic resources contribute differently to meaning.
- Modes have different **affordances**, so analysis should ask not only what is represented but why a given resource is suitable for representing it.
- The **textual metafunction** concerns organisation and coherence: layout, reading order, mode choice, size, scale, and composition.
- The **ideational metafunction** concerns represented content: processes, participants, circumstances, and whether visual structures are narrative or conceptual.
- Relations between modes can be **redundant, complementary/extending, elaborating, or contrasting**. Meaning can therefore lie in the relation between image and writing rather than in either mode alone.
- The **interpersonal metafunction** concerns relations between author, represented content, and reader, including speech roles, formality, and explicit or implicit value-positioning.
- The framework is designed to expose how meaning is made across resources rather than assuming that one mode simply illustrates another.

**Concepts useful for LaclauGPT:** This paper provides a strong methodology for the **pre-discourse multimodal first pass**. It can ground frame and summary prompts in social semiotics before Laclauian discourse analysis begins. Useful concepts include semiotic resources, mode, affordance, salience/composition, processes/participants/circumstances, narrative versus conceptual representation, reading order, image–text relations, and cautious description of positioning. The output of this stage should preserve salient signifiers and relations without yet classifying nodal points, empty/floating signifiers, chains of equivalence, antagonisms, populism, ideology, or hegemonic formations.

A suitable conceptual pipeline is:

```text
raw image/video/text/audio/metadata
    ↓
multimodal social-semiotic description
    ↓
structuralist representation of signs and relations
    ↓
LaclauGPT discourse analysis
```

This creates a clean bridge to **LLM Structuralism**. Saussure supplies relational signification and difference; Barthes can contribute the practical distinction between denotation and culturally available connotation; social semiotics supplies the multimodal methodology; Laclau then analyzes political articulation. The pre-analysis should therefore identify what signs are present, how they are combined, which modes carry them, what contrasts and associations are observable, and where interpretation is uncertain, while leaving political/discourse interpretation to later stages.

**Suggested first-pass schema for LaclauGPT:**

1. **Denotative description:** what is literally visible, audible, written, spoken, or otherwise present.
2. **Semiotic resources / modes:** linguistic, visual, auditory, spatial, gestural, typographic, editing/montage, interface/platform elements.
3. **Salient signs/signifiers:** repeated, foregrounded, unusually prominent, or explicitly emphasized words, objects, symbols, sounds, gestures, and visual elements. Record them descriptively, without assigning Laclaudian status.
4. **Processes, participants, circumstances:** who/what appears, what happens, and where/when/how it happens.
5. **Composition and salience:** foreground/background, centre/periphery, size, scale, sequencing, reading/viewing order, repetition, visual/sonic emphasis.
6. **Intermodal relations:** whether modes repeat, extend, elaborate, anchor, or contrast with one another.
7. **Connotation / culturally available associations:** only when strongly supported by the material; keep denotation and interpretation explicitly separated.
8. **Ambiguity and uncertainty:** identify unclear references, OCR/transcription uncertainty, ambiguous symbols, missing context, and competing plausible readings.
9. **Neutral frame description:** summarize the principal way the material organizes attention and meaning, without political classification or discourse analysis.
10. **Neutral multimodal summary:** preserve salient signs, actors, actions, quoted/visible text, and cross-modal relations for downstream analysis.

**Relevance to `paper/PHASE_1_PAPER.md`:** High methodological relevance. Phase 1 currently depends on a first-pass summary/frame layer before discourse analysis. Giving that layer an explicit social-semiotic methodology makes the complete pipeline theoretically interpretable rather than treating multimodal preprocessing as a generic LLM description task. This is especially important for social-media data where speech, captions, typography, music, editing, screenshots, reaction video, memes, platform UI, and visual symbolism may jointly produce the source material later analyzed by LaclauGPT.

**Limitations / cautions:** The paper's empirical framework was designed for static multimodal educational texts, and the authors explicitly delimit their use of “multimodal texts” from animations. Applying it to TikTok, Instagram, video, audio, memes, screenshots, and other born-digital material is therefore an extension of the framework, not something directly validated by this study. LaclauGPT should retain the framework's core principles while adding temporal sequence, editing/montage, speech/prosody, music/sound, gesture, and platform-interface resources. The first pass must also avoid turning “positioning” or “implicit values” into premature political inference.

**Critical methodological assessment:** The framework is valuable less because it supplies a single coding taxonomy than because it forces the analyst to keep three questions distinct: how a text is organised, what kinds of processes/participants/circumstances it represents, and how the producer-reader relation is configured. Table 1 is the operational heart of the paper. It combines textual organisation and scale, ideational transitivity plus narrative/conceptual image structure and image-writing relations, and interpersonal positioning/speech roles/formality/values. This makes the method unusually suitable for an evidence-preserving preprocessing contract: each observation can remain attached to a modality and source location instead of being collapsed into a free-form summary.

**What should and should not transfer to LaclauGPT:** The strongest transferable components are resource inventory, affordance, transitivity, composition, visual vectors, reading/viewing order, and image-writing relations. The weakest direct transfer is the paper's interpersonal interpretation of author positioning and implicit values. In an educational assessment setting this is a legitimate analytical target; in LaclauGPT it risks leaking political interpretation into preprocessing. Phase 1 therefore retains only directly observable interpersonal form and defers political positioning, ideology, hegemony, populism and Laclaudian categories.

**Validity and scope:** The empirical demonstration is deliberately small: one Grade-7 ecology student text and one digital chemistry teaching resource. That is enough to illustrate the framework but not to validate it as a universal multimodal coding instrument. The article's contribution is methodological and illustrative, not a reliability study or large-sample benchmark. It does not report inter-coder reliability for the proposed complete framework. LLM schema consistency and model reproducibility therefore have to be evaluated independently.

**Born-digital extension:** The paper explicitly defines its multimodal texts as paper-based or digital static texts and excludes animations. LaclauGPT therefore treats temporal sequence, editing/montage, speech/prosody, music/sound, gesture, subtitles, OCR/ASR, reaction layouts and platform/interface cues as an explicit extension. These additions follow social-semiotic logic but are not empirical claims made by Wanselin et al.

**Source audit:** The bibliography was checked against publisher or institutional records for the principal sources that carry the framework. The article metadata and DOI match Springer. Martinec & Salway (2005) matches Visual Communication 4(3):337-371 and DOI 10.1177/1470357205055928; Tang, Won & Treagust (2019) matches International Journal of Science Education 41(16):2296-2322 and DOI 10.1080/09500693.2019.1672906; Danielsson (2016) matches Linguistics and Education 35:88-99 and DOI 10.1016/j.linged.2016.07.005; Bezemer & Kress (2008) matches Written Communication 25(2):166-195 and DOI 10.1177/0741088307313177; Black & Wiliam (2009), Preston (2018), and Wennersten et al. (2020) also match the cited publication records. No obvious fabricated or materially mis-cited reference was found in this targeted check. This is a verification of the framework's principal and selected empirical references, not an exhaustive bibliometric audit of every citation.

**Phase 1 implementation decision:** The canonical contract is social-semiotic-preanalysis.v1. It separates denotation, resource inventory, participants/processes/circumstances, composition, salient source-form signs, observable sign relations, intermodal relations, cautious connotation, cross-modal synthesis, and typed uncertainty. It explicitly rejects extra structured fields for downstream political/discourse categories. Prompt resources are versioned as multimodal.system:v2, multimodal.frame_analysis:v2, and multimodal.summary_analysis:v2; historical v1 prompts remain loadable for reproducibility.

**Potential follow-up:** In Phase 1, redesign `puhti_frame.py` and `puhti_summary.py` prompts as a modality-agnostic **Multimodal Social-Semiotic Pre-Analysis** stage. Document the methodology in the Phase 1 paper and general documentation once implementation is validated. Add tests ensuring that the first pass remains descriptive and does not perform Laclauian, ideological, partisan, populism, sentiment, or political classification that belongs downstream.
