## Research watch

### High signal: public AI-risk discourse is becoming politically mainstream

A Reuters/Ipsos poll published 22 September reports that 73% of U.S. respondents worry AI companies are not doing enough to prevent potentially catastrophic consequences, 55% support slowing AI development, and respondents strongly prefer safe/responsible development over global competitiveness. This matters for Phase 1 because existential-risk and safety signifiers are no longer confined to specialist communities; they are entering mass political opinion and may articulate with distrust of corporate self-regulation and demands for federal oversight.

At the same time, Donald Trump publicly rejected new AI regulation while saying the Justice Department could intervene if needed and framing AI as a transformation larger than the Industrial Revolution. UN Secretary-General António Guterres called for multilateral AI risk governance and independent oversight. These positions make `safety`, `regulation`, `competition`, `progress`, `catastrophe`, `authority` and `superintelligence` especially valuable floating/contested signifiers to follow empirically rather than treating actors as fixed ideological types.

### High signal: the pacing fight acquires legal form — *Buist et al. v. Anthropic*

The subscriber antitrust suit noted yesterday now has a name and a concrete legal theory: *Buist et al. v. Anthropic PBC et al.* (N.D. Cal., No. 3:26-cv-10693, filed 18 September) alleges that coordinated public endorsements of AI slowdowns constitute an output-restricting cartel under Sherman Act §1, regardless of the safety rationale. It was filed six days after Amodei's "We Must Pace the Frontier" essay drew same-day endorsements from Altman, Musk and Hassabis, and the legislative escape route closed the same week when Hawley and Cruz blocked a national-security antitrust exemption in the NDAA package. The labs face a structural binary: abandon safety coordination or defend it in court.

**Why this matters for AI26:** an ideological antagonism between safety coordination and acceleration is being articulated *as law*. The suit converts a discourse coalition into a formal defendant set, and signifiers such as `pacing`, `coordination`, `collusion`, `safety` and `output restriction` are being redefined by actors in materially different positions. Strong candidate material for the Phase 1 corpus and for discourse-coalition tracing in Phase 2.

### High signal: US–China bilateral talks precede a Trump–Xi governance meeting

US and Chinese officials met on 20–21 September to discuss AI governance, reportedly including AI incident-notification mechanisms, ahead of a Trump–Xi meeting expected to take up AI on 24 September. This unfolds against Guterres's UNGA-week push for multilateral risk governance and the UN panel's 21 September brief on autonomous agents. The analytically interesting part is the framing collision rather than any communiqué: US discourse currently oscillates between "existential race" and "hoax", while Chinese official discourse treats advanced AI as powerful but governable — loss-of-control scenarios written into official safety frameworks, agent-behaviour control guidelines, comprehensive AI legislation on the 2026 legislative agenda — and state media reads Amodei's pacing essay as a hypocritical "AI Cold War script".

**Why this matters for AI26:** the same loss-of-control concern is being articulated into nearly opposite institutional imaginaries — multilateral oversight, state containment, and market acceleration. That contrast speaks directly to the sociotechnical-imaginaries dimension of the locked Phase 1 framework and to any future comparative sampling decision.

### Background-to-signal: California builds an audit profession while Washington blocks coordination

California signed SB 813 (voluntary independent-verification organisations, to be certified by 2028) and AB 1405 (auditor registry and independence standards). Governor Newsom's 18 September executive order convenes an expert group to propose state-level AI safety law within two months, explicitly considering independent frontier monitors, safety plans and a "kill switch" emergency-shutoff requirement. Further bills await the governor's 30 September signature deadline, and Colorado's revised ADMT rules were expected today. Meanwhile the federal executive dismisses safety as a "hoax" and the Senate blocks the antitrust waiver.

**Why this matters for AI26:** the emerging US configuration is not "regulation vs no regulation" but a division of articulatory labour — a state audit/verification profession, an antitrust challenge to safety coordination, and a hostile federal executive. If independent verification organisations become institutional actors, they will enter the discourse network as claim-makers in their own right. The 30 September signature decisions are a natural sampling moment.

### High signal for validation: auditable local/human-centred workflows

Jeldtoft and Yousef's 2026 preprint on auditable LLM-supported thematic analysis is unusually compatible with LaclauGPT's methodological direction: preserve interpretive context, maintain traceable source-output links, represent analytical constructs explicitly, constrain LLM inference to interpretive tasks, and support privacy-preserving local deployment. Their evaluation combines structural comparison with human analysis and independent expert assessment rather than relying on agreement alone.

Zupic's 2026 human-centred content-analysis workflow similarly treats annotation, summarisation and extraction as researcher-designed, supervised and validated stages. For Phase 1, these papers support modular provenance and human adjudication; they do not justify replacing the locked Laclaudian method with thematic/content analysis.

One refinement to the 20 September note on Liu et al., *Agreement Is Not Quality* (arXiv:2607.28890): its most actionable contribution for the Phase 1 pilot is the code-level division-of-labor framework — six codes automatable, twelve better served by LLM coding, fifteen demonstrably requiring human expertise, and sixteen suited to confidence-based triage — together with a blind-verification protocol that treats human and LLM code sets symmetrically. This supports planning validation per code rather than per model, with blinded adjudication instead of agreement alone.

On the regulatory side, the Dutch DPA's ~€966M Uber fine is being read as a GDPR Article 22 precedent that human oversight of automated and agentic decisions must be designed in before deployment — a governance echo of the same human-in-the-loop principle LaclauGPT applies to its own LLM pre-analysis.

For Phase 2 specifically, Randerson et al.'s 2026 DNA study is directly relevant: GPT-4 recovered more than two-thirds of researcher concepts and screened irrelevant material well, but generated enough irrelevant concepts that human review remained necessary. This is a strong empirical warning against allowing an LLM to autonomously construct the DNA codebook or coalition structure.
